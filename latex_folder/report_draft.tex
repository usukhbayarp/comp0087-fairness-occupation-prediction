% ── ABSTRACT ────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
We study gender bias in occupation prediction using the Bias-in-Bios
dataset, comparing Pythia generative models (160M--1.4B) and encoder
classifiers (DistilBERT, RoBERTa) under masked and unmasked conditions.
Against the common intuition that larger models are fairer, we find that
unsupervised scaling \emph{amplifies} bias in the Pythia family on this task.
Gender masking partially mitigates fairness gaps in encoder models but
produces backfire effects in generative models, indicating that proxy
representations may be model-specific rather than a universal property of
the input text.
Token-level attribution further suggests that indirect contextual signals,
not solely explicit gender markers, appear to be the dominant carriers of the gender
signal --- and that they persist even after masking.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. INTRODUCTION ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Language models encode and reproduce societal biases present in their
training data \citep{caliskan-etal-2017-bias}. When deployed in
high-stakes settings, such biases can translate into systematic
disparities that disadvantage particular demographic groups.

Occupation prediction is a revealing case study for this problem.
Models trained on real-world career data tend to associate
professions like \emph{nurse} or \emph{dietitian} with female-typical
language, and \emph{surgeon} or \emph{software engineer} with
male-typical patterns. \citet{de-arteaga-etal-2019-bias} showed, using
the Bias-in-Bios dataset, that scrubbing explicit gender signals does
not remove these disparities: implicit proxy features survive.
Yet the identity of these proxy features — which tokens are associated
with the gender signal, how their salience changes under masking, and
whether this varies with model scale or model type — remains poorly
understood.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Proxy bias is not solely driven by explicit gender markers.} On the Bias-in-Bios task, gender-correlated
  contextual tokens dominate attribution scores by a large margin over
  explicit gender words, while remaining active even after gender masking.
  To our knowledge, this provides one of the first detailed token-level
  attribution analyses of this kind on this dataset.
  \item \textbf{Masking effectiveness depends on training regime, not architecture alone.} Explicit gender masking reduces bias in encoder models and in zero/few-shot generative settings, but proves ineffective—and sometimes harmful—after generative fine-tuning. Attribution stability further shows that proxy representations differ between individual models (e.g.\ DistilBERT vs.\ RoBERTa) even between closely related encoder models, providing evidence that proxy structure may be model-specific rather than a fixed property of the input text.
  \item \textbf{Unsupervised scaling amplifies bias; supervision
  recovers it.} Across Pythia model sizes (160M--1.4B), zero-shot bias
  grows substantially with scale, while fine-tuning consistently
  restores fairness. Both findings hold across masked and unmasked
  conditions on this dataset, while we believe the pattern of
  capacity-driven bias amplification could extend to other settings.
\end{itemize}

% ── 2. RELATED WORK ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Bias in occupation prediction.}
\citet{de-arteaga-etal-2019-bias} introduced Bias-in-Bios and showed that
classifiers exhibit gender disparities even after pronoun scrubbing.
\citet{romanov-etal-2019-whats} examined the limits of debiasing through
scrubbing. Our work extends this line of research by identifying and
quantifying the specific proxy tokens responsible, using gradient-based
attribution.

\textbf{Fairness metrics.}
\citet{hardt2016equality} formalised Equalized Odds and
\citet{dwork2012fairness} introduced demographic parity. We evaluate both
using a one-vs-rest multiclass extension macro-averaged over occupations.

\textbf{Attribution methods.}
\citet{sundararajan2017axiomatic} introduced Integrated Gradients (IG),
which satisfies completeness and sensitivity axioms and is the standard
choice for token-level analysis of sequence classifiers
\citep{attanasio2022entropy}; its application to occupation
classification is limited.

\textbf{Model scaling and fairness.}
\citet{bommasani2021opportunities} discusses bias amplification as a risk
in large foundation models. Our scaling study gives concrete evidence of
this in the Pythia family: zero-shot bias increases with model size.

\textbf{Gender masking.}
\citet{lu-etal-2020-gender} study counterfactual data augmentation for
gender bias. Unlike that work, we also measure how masking affects
token-level attribution patterns, giving an internal view of how models
redistribute feature importance when gender signals are removed.

\textbf{Debiasing methods.}
Proposed approaches include adversarial training \citep{zhao-etal-2018-gender},
data augmentation \citep{lu-etal-2020-gender}, and post-hoc embedding projection
\citep{sun-etal-2019-mitigating}.
\citet{gonen-goldberg-2019-lipstick} showed such methods often conceal rather
than remove bias, motivating diagnostic analysis of which features carry
residual signals --- the complementary perspective we take here.

% ── 3. DATASET ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}

\subsection{Bias-in-Bios}
We use the Bias-in-Bios dataset \citep{de-arteaga-etal-2019-bias}
(\texttt{LabHC/bias\_in\_bios}), a standard benchmark for studying
occupational stereotypes and evaluating debiasing methods in NLP.
Each example consists of a short biography (\texttt{hard\_text}) paired
with an integer profession label and a binary gender attribute inferred
from pronoun usage by the original authors (0\,$\to$\,M, 1\,$\to$\,F).
For example: \textit{``Dr. K. Rama Krishna practices at Pet Park Clinic 
in Begumpet, Hyderabad. He completed B.V.Sc & A.H from College of Veterinary
 Science, Rajendranagar, SVVU in 2009.''} (label: \emph{physician}, gender: M).
In practice, models may produce biased outputs on similar inputs.
For instance, a biography describing caregiving activities may be predicted as
\emph{nurse} when written with female-associated language, but as \emph{physician}
when similar qualifications are expressed with male-associated language.
This illustrates how learned associations, rather than qualifications alone,
can influence predictions.
The dataset contains 381,807 examples (train 248,141 / dev 38,198 /
test 95,468), with a near-identical gender split of $\approx\!46.2\%$
female across all partitions, indicating stratified sampling.
We restrict to the \textbf{top 20 most frequent occupations} in the
training split. The task is to predict occupation from biography text
alone; gender is used only for post-hoc fairness evaluation.

Table~\ref{tab:professions} shows the per-profession counts and gender
distribution in the training split. Several occupations exhibit strong
gender skew: \emph{dietitian} (92.9\% F), \emph{nurse} (90.8\% F),
and \emph{model} (82.7\% F) are heavily female-dominated, while
\emph{surgeon} (14.8\% F), \emph{software engineer} (15.8\% F), and
\emph{composer} (16.4\% F) are heavily male-dominated.

\subsection{Evaluation Subset}
All models are evaluated on a \textbf{3,000-sample test subset},
stratified proportionally by profession from the 95,468-example test
split; IG cost (32 forward passes per example) precluded the full test set.
Per-profession counts range from 932 (professor) to 27
(comedian), so results for rare occupations should be read with caution.

\subsection{Gender Masking}
We evaluate all models under two conditions: \textbf{Unmasked}
(original text) and \textbf{Masked}, where gender tokens are replaced
with the model's native mask token (\texttt{[MASK]} for DistilBERT,
\texttt{<mask>} for RoBERTa, and the literal string \texttt{[MASK]}
for Pythia, which as a causal language model has no native mask token)
using a case-insensitive word-boundary regex. Three categories of tokens are masked: pronouns and possessives
(\emph{he, she, his, her}, etc.), titles and honorifics (\emph{mr,
mrs, ms, sir, lady}, etc.), and 54 gendered nouns (\emph{father,
mother, husband, wife, actor, actress}, etc.). Names are not masked, as they carry biographical content unrelated
to gender and reliable name-matching produces too many false positives.

% ── 4. METHODS ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Methods}

\subsection{Pythia: Zero-shot and Few-shot}
We evaluate Pythia \citep{biderman2023pythia} at three scales (160M,
410M, and 1.4B parameters) under zero-shot (biography plus candidate
label list) and few-shot ($K\!=\!4$ exemplar pairs) regimes, both with
and without gender masking.\footnote{The four exemplars cover professor (female pronoun), physician (male pronoun), journalist (male pronoun), and attorney (female pronoun), giving a balanced 2:2 female-to-male ratio.}
As a causal language model, Pythia scores each candidate label $c_k$
by its joint log-probability given the prompt $p$:
\begin{equation} \log P(c_k \mid p) = \sum_{t=1}^{|c_k|} \log P\!\left(c_k^{(t)} \mid p, c_k^{(1)}, \ldots, c_k^{(t-1)}\right) \end{equation}
The predicted label is $\hat{y} = \arg\max_{c_k} \log P(c_k \mid p)$.

\subsection{Pythia: Supervised Fine-tuning}
Pythia is fine-tuned as a sequence classifier (with a linear head over the final hidden state) using LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} on the attention projections ($r=16$, $\alpha=32$, dropout 0.1). We train for 3 epochs with a batch size of 16 and a max length of 256. The learning rate is $3 \times 10^{-4}$ for Pythia 160M and $2 \times 10^{-5}$ otherwise. Checkpoints are selected by the development set Macro-F1. For the 1.4B variant, 4-bit QLoRA is utilized.
\subsection{Encoder Baselines: DistilBERT and RoBERTa}
DistilBERT \citep{sanh2019distilbert} (66M) and RoBERTa-base
\citep{liu2019roberta} (125M) are fine-tuned as sequence classifiers
with a linear head, lr $2\!\times\!10^{-5}$, batch 16, 3 epochs,
max length 256, best checkpoint by Macro-F1. The same pipeline runs
for both masked and unmasked conditions with a shared label mapping.

\subsection{Proxy Bias Attribution}
We apply Integrated Gradients \citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic} to the
two fine-tuned encoder models, computing attributions toward the
\emph{predicted} class index.
We use \textbf{32 interpolation steps} and a \textbf{zero-embedding
baseline}. Token-level scores are obtained by summing attribution
values across the embedding dimension for each token position.
Before top-$K$ selection, subword tokens are merged to word level:
BERT-style \texttt{\#\#} continuation pieces and RoBERTa's
whitespace-prefixed BPE tokens are each handled separately, with
scores summed across pieces. We keep only tokens with positive
attribution scores and a minimum length of 2 characters, with a
maximum sequence length of \textbf{256 tokens}.

The pipeline runs in two stages. In \textbf{Stage 1} ($K_1\!=\!5$),
the 5 highest-scoring positive tokens are extracted per example. In
\textbf{Stage 2}, these are aggregated across all examples for each
profession (minimum token length 3, stopwords removed) to compute a
weighted score:
\begin{equation}
  \text{wscore}(t,p) = \text{count\_topk}(t,p) \times \overline{\text{attr}}(t,p)
\end{equation}
where $\text{count\_topk}(t,p)$ is the number of examples where token
$t$ appears in the stage-1 top-5 for profession $p$, and
$\overline{\text{attr}}(t,p)$ is the mean attribution score.
We attribute toward the \emph{predicted} label rather than the true
label throughout, since the goal is to understand what the model relies
on, not whether it is correct \citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}.
Note that gendered pronouns (\emph{he}, \emph{she}, \emph{his},
\emph{her}, etc.) are included in the Stage~2 stopword list and
therefore never appear in the aggregated proxy tables, regardless of
masking condition. The gender signal comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:gender_tokens} thus reflects only non-pronoun gendered
vocabulary (titles, gendered nouns). Faithfulness testing is unaffected,
as it uses the Stage~1 top-$k$ tokens which retain pronouns.

\textbf{Stability analysis.}
To compare masked and unmasked attribution patterns, we take the
\textbf{top-10 aggregated tokens} per profession ($K_2\!=\!10$) and
compute a shared token ratio:
\begin{equation}
  r(p) = \frac{|\mathcal{T}_\text{sh}|}{
    |\mathcal{T}_\text{sh}|+|\mathcal{T}_\text{m}|+|\mathcal{T}_\text{u}|}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_\text{sh}$, $\mathcal{T}_\text{m}$,
$\mathcal{T}_\text{u}$ are the tokens shared between conditions,
masked-only, and unmasked-only respectively.
Gender-conditioned proxy tables are produced using the same procedure,
stratified by profession and gender.

\textbf{Faithfulness validation.}
To check whether the attributed tokens are functionally important for
the prediction, we erase the top-5 tokens from each example's text and
re-run the model, measuring the resulting probability drop:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Faith}(x) = P(\hat{y} \mid x) - P(\hat{y} \mid x_{\text{erased}})
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}$ is the predicted label for example $x$.
Attribution scores are correlational: high attribution indicates token
salience under the learned representation, not causal responsibility.
Erasure provides limited interventional support but is best read as
local functional dependence rather than proof of mechanism.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate on both task performance and fairness. Let $Y$ be the true
occupation label, $\hat{Y}$ the predicted label, $A \in \{M, F\}$ the
binary gender attribute, and $\mathcal{C}$ the set of 20 occupation classes.

\textbf{Task performance.} We report Accuracy and Macro-F1 to account for
class imbalance.

\textbf{Fairness metrics.} We measure gender disparities using one-vs-rest
multiclass extensions of Demographic Parity (DP) and Equalized Odds (EO),
macro-averaged across occupations \citep{hardt2016equality,dwork2012fairness}.
For binary gender $A\in\{M,F\}$ and label set $\mathcal{C}$:
{\small
\begin{align*}
\Delta\text{DP}  &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\!\sum_{c}\bigl|
    P(\hat{Y}{=}c\mid A{=}M) - P(\hat{Y}{=}c\mid A{=}F)\bigr|\\[2pt]
\Delta\text{TPR} &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\!\sum_{c}\bigl|
    P(\hat{Y}{=}c\mid Y{=}c,A{=}M)\\
    &\phantom{{}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\!\sum_{c}\bigl|}
    {}-P(\hat{Y}{=}c\mid Y{=}c,A{=}F)\bigr|\\[2pt]
\Delta\text{FPR} &= \tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\!\sum_{c}\bigl|
    P(\hat{Y}{=}c\mid Y{\neq}c,A{=}M)\\
    &\phantom{{}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\!\sum_{c}\bigl|}
    {}-P(\hat{Y}{=}c\mid Y{\neq}c,A{=}F)\bigr|
\end{align*}}
The EO gap is $\max(\Delta\text{TPR},\,\Delta\text{FPR})$.

Fine-tuned models consistently show lower EO gaps than their zero-shot counterparts. Unmasked zero-shot EO grows from $\sim$0.09 at 160M to $\sim$0.21 at 1.4B, showing that unsupervised scaling amplifies bias in the Pythia family on this task.

Crucially, the effectiveness of gender masking depends heavily on the learning regime. We observe a large masking benefit for Pythia in zero-shot and few-shot settings (e.g., Pythia-1.4B 0s: 0.209$\to$0.134; Pythia-410M 0s: 0.135$\to$0.095; Pythia-1.4B FS: 0.120$\to$0.083), though zero-shot bias still grows with scale even when masked.

In contrast, masking produces a comparatively smaller difference for the fine-tuned encoder models (RoBERTa: 0.082 to 0.063; DistilBERT: 0.088 to 0.071). Furthermore, at the fine-tuned level for Pythia, masking produces backfire effects at the profession level (e.g., for \emph{teacher}, \emph{poet}, and \emph{painter}), with Pythia-410M FT showing a small aggregate increase in bias (0.085 to 0.088).

Finally, the FPR gap is consistently much smaller than the TPR gap across all models, so TPR disparity remains the dominant fairness concern. Taken together, these results present a nuanced picture: while masking explicit gender tokens successfully disrupts bias when relying on raw, pre-trained representations (zero/few-shot), it is still non-zero, and insufficient once a model is explicitly fine-tuned. During fine-tuning, bias appears to redistribute across broader contextual co-occurrence patterns, rendering the simple removal of explicit markers ineffective.

Gender-conditioned analysis shows partial asymmetry.

nurse$|$M at 6 (ratio 0.43), a modest asymmetry suggesting the model's
feature space for \emph{nurse} is slightly more anchored in
female-typical contextual patterns.
This aligns with the fairness results in Section~\ref{sec:fairness},
where \emph{nurse} shows one of the largest EO gap reductions under
masking, suggesting that professions with less stable proxy
representations benefit more from masking.

predictive load once gender signals are removed.

% ── 7. CONCLUSION ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

Fine-tuned models outperform zero/few-shot baselines on both
performance and fairness. Scaling without supervision amplifies bias:
zero-shot EO gap grows from $\sim$0.09 at 160M to $\sim$0.21 at 1.4B,
showing that model capacity alone is not a substitute for fine-tuning
or explicit debiasing. Masking reduces aggregate EO in encoder models
(by 0.017--0.019), although this effect is close to the estimated
sampling variability ($\approx\!0.018$) and should be interpreted as a
weak but consistent trend rather than a robust improvement; masking
produces backfire effects at the profession level in Pythia. Attribution analysis suggests that gender-correlated information is
encoded mainly through indirect contextual tokens; their salience is
model-specific and their functional importance is supported by erasure
faithfulness testing.
Gender-conditioned analysis adds a further observation: male nurse
predictions are concentrated in a critically small feature set, with
faithfulness nearly double that of female nurse predictions
(0.756 vs.\ 0.354 under DistilBERT).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Gender labels in Bias-in-Bios are binary and inferred from pronoun
usage \citep{de-arteaga-etal-2019-bias}, which does not reflect the
full spectrum of gender identity and constrains fairness evaluation to
a male/female binary. Attribution is restricted to encoder models.
Names were not masked (reliable name--gender matching produces too many false positives and names carry legitimate biographical content); however, since names are gender-correlated, they may act as direct gender signals rather than proxies. The attribution findings therefore capture proxy patterns among non-name tokens, and the claim that proxy bias is carried by indirect contextual signals should be understood with this caveat.
Attribution scores are correlational, not causal: they show which
features the trained model appears to rely on, not why those features
became encoded during training. Our erasure-based faithfulness test
provides limited interventional evidence, but because token removal
alters the input distribution and may produce unrealistic texts, the
resulting confidence drop is best interpreted as evidence of functional
dependence rather than a definitive causal test. Strong causal
claims would require controlled counterfactual interventions,
which are beyond the scope of this study. Per-profession sample sizes are small for rare occupations (comedian
$n\!=\!27$, dietitian $n\!=\!30$), making per-profession TPR estimates
unreliable for those classes.
Macro-averaging gives an aggregate EO standard error of $\approx\!0.018$;
the larger zero-shot gaps ($\sim\!0.09$--$0.21$) are well above this
noise floor, while fine-tuned differences remain close to it.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Extending attribution analysis to generative models would provide
deeper explanations of the fairness disparities observed for Pythia.
Developing reliable uncertainty estimates for the EO gap remains
open: standard bootstrap CIs degrade under the \texttt{max()} operator
and severe class imbalance, so analytical bounds or
permutation-based tests may be more appropriate here.
A natural next step for causal analysis would be controlled
counterfactual biography editing or causal mediation analysis over
specific token groups.
